2. Motivation

Going out in New York is a research project.

Eater for the restaurant. The Infatuation to check it. Resident Advisor for who is playing. A listings site for the gallery opening. A ticket page to see if anything is left. Then a map, to find out whether any of it is near any of the rest. Nine tabs, four opinions that disagree, and a decision still to make.

The cost is not the time. It is the night that does not happen.

The research is tiring enough that the easy answer wins: the same bar as last time, chosen at eleven o'clock because it was the only option still in your head. That is not a preference. It is what decision fatigue looks like from the inside, and the city you moved here for goes unvisited while it happens.

It is worse the more specific you are.

A table for eight needs somewhere that will take eight. A second date needs somewhere you can hear each other. A birthday needs a room that feels like one. None of the listings sites let you ask for any of that, so the people with the most particular needs do the most work.

And nothing puts the pieces together.

Each of those nine tabs answers one question. Where to eat. What is on. Whether there are tickets left. None of them answers the actual question, which is what the whole evening looks like: where to start, what comes after, whether the two are anywhere near each other, and whether it adds up to a night rather than a list of places.

Who this is for.

Anyone going out in New York, but sharply: groups who need a room that fits them, people new to a neighbourhood, anyone who has noticed they keep going to the same three places and would like to stop.

We plan a night, not a venue.

This is the difference that matters most, and it is the one every product above shares. They return options: a list, a feed, a ranked set of bars. The deciding, the ordering and the working out of whether any of it fits together is still left to you, which is precisely the work that was exhausting in the first place. The Programme returns a complete evening. A gallery opening at six, dinner at half seven, a small room with a DJ at eleven, somewhere for a sweet treat after. Different kinds of activity, in a sensible order, timed so one finishes before the next begins, close enough together to walk, and chosen to match a single stated vibe rather than assembled from separate searches. 19 kinds of evening can appear in one plan. Nobody else crosses categories. A nightlife app will not book your dinner. A restaurant guide does not know what is on at the venue round the corner. A ticketing platform sells you one event and stops. Stitching those together is the job people are actually doing by hand across nine tabs, and it is the job we took.

We model the room, not the listing.

Every product above filters on genre, date, neighbourhood and price. None of them know how big the room is. But a night in a space that holds eighty is not a night in one that holds three thousand, and a yard in July is not the same place in January. We hold the capacity of each room, whether it is indoors or out, which of a venue's several spaces you would be standing in, and whether it will seat a party of twelve. Nowadays is not one venue to us: it is a 269-capacity indoor room and an 800-capacity yard, and they are different nights.

We sequence, and we respect travel.

A plan is stops in order, with real walking times between them, and a bias toward keeping the night tight. Less time on the train means more of the evening spent somewhere. Anyone who wants to range across the city can say so and the radius opens up.

We are biased against the obvious.

A recommendation you could have made yourself is not worth making. The planner shuffles among the strongest matches rather than returning the same famous answer every time, and the corpus is built from local and niche coverage as well as the national titles.

We do not pretend to certainties we do not have.

A room whose capacity nobody has checked is never offered to a group. A venue that only opens for ticketed events says so rather than implying you can walk in. A booking link is shown only once we have fetched the page and confirmed it names that venue.

4. The agents

Seven agents across two tiers. The split is the main design decision in the project and the one worth defending: the expensive work runs once, offline, and is shared by every user, rather than being repeated for each visitor. What remains at request time is the work a database genuinely cannot do: reading what someone meant, judging whether the result is any good, and understanding an amendment to a plan already on screen.

The Planner is deliberately **not** an agent, and that is the decision this section exists to argue. It is also why the Critic exists: a deterministic planner is fast and predictable and structurally unable to notice that its own output does not work as an evening. Judgment is the one thing it cannot do, so that is exactly the job given to a model. Choosing venues that match a request is filtering. Sequencing them is arithmetic. The prose about a venue does not change between visitors, so it is written once and cached. None of that needs a language model, so none of it uses one. What remains for a model at request time is reading the sentence someone typed, and often not even that.

Models, and why

`gpt-5.6-luna` throughout, through a direct OpenAI client. The assignment names `gpt-6-luna` on the course Portkey key; that key was rejected by the gateway on every authentication method tried, and the instructor has already approved the substitution for coursework. The app still supports both and falls back automatically, so a grader with a working course key is covered either way. Every agent returns a schema-validated object rather than free text.

What we did to cut cost

  1. Move the expensive agents offline. The Scout, Researcher and Writer run on a refresh, not per visitor. Their cost is paid once and amortised across every plan ever served.
  2. Do not use a model for work that is not language work. Filtering and sequencing became ordinary Python. This removed roughly 2,000 output tokens from every single request.
  3. Skip the Concierge when the form already says it. A complete set of filters is an unambiguous request. Re-reading it with a model is a round trip that buys nothing.
  4. Keep the one remaining prompt stable so it caches. The Concierge instructions do not change between requests, so they are served from the provider's prompt cache.
  5. Reach for web search only when the corpus cannot answer. Search is the single most expensive thing the app can do, in both money and latency. It is a fallback, not a default.

Did it make the app worse?

Yes, and it is worth stating plainly. Venue copy is now written once per venue rather than per request, so a card's description does not know that this particular visitor said "birthday, eight of us". The connective sentence is still written per request and the structural facts still respond to the brief, but the prose is general. This is the same tradeoff a city guide makes. The Infatuation writes one review per restaurant, not one per reader. We judged a one-second plan with general prose to be worth more than a twenty-five-second plan with bespoke prose, and the measured difference is below. The second cost is coverage. The fast path can only recommend what is in the corpus, so a request the dataset does not cover falls through to the model and takes roughly twenty seconds instead of two.

6. Safety

The app reads third-party editorial pages, venue listings and live search results, and it shows people links they are expected to click. That makes prompt injection the central risk rather than a bolted-on one, and a wrong booking link the most expensive failure. Every defence below is exercised by `test_guardrails.py`, which runs them all and reports pass or fail.

A defence that was wrong, found by a real run

The first version of the booking-link allowlist blocked `calendar.houseofyes.org` — House of Yes's own booking page. Over-blocking is not safety; it removes the useful half of the product and trains people to go and find the link somewhere less careful. The check now asks a different question: not "is this on my list" but "is this pretending to be on my list". A venue's own domain passes. `resy.com.secure-booking.io` does not.

A link that resolved, and was still wrong

A plan came back with a Book a table button pointing at `opentable.com/restaurant/profile/1394212`. It returned HTTP 200, so nothing downstream complained. Nothing confirmed it was the right restaurant either, and it was not: the Editor had produced it from memory. A link that resolves but opens the wrong venue is worse than no link, because nobody finds out until they arrive. Booking links are now shown only when the page has been fetched and confirmed to name that venue.

A critic that catches what the filters cannot

The planner matched categories, respected every filter and kept the stops close together, and still produced an evening with a three-hour hole in it between dinner and an eleven o'clock dance floor. No filter can express that objection. The Critic reads the finished plan and says so in the terms a person would, and the planner rebuilds without the stop it named.

Failing without losing the plan

An invented venue used to fail schema validation and take the whole evening down with it, returning a 500. The model's answer now lands in a lenient draft; a repair step fills what it can from our own data and drops only what it cannot attribute. One missing fact costs one stop, not the night.

8. Limits and next steps

What does not work yet

  • Opening hours are not checked. The planner assigns times from a table of typical hours. It does not know that a restaurant closes on Mondays. OpenStreetMap returns an `opening_hours` field free alongside the geocoding we already do, and collecting it is the single most valuable next piece of work.
  • No event calendar. The app knows venues, not what is on at them tonight. For a ticketed room it can only say "check what is on", which is honest but thin. A Ticketmaster Discovery feed would close most of this.
  • Coverage is uneven. 507 of 2173 venues have coordinates, so the rest show no map and no walking time. OpenStreetMap's coverage of small businesses is patchy, and worst in the outer boroughs.
  • Some kinds of night are thinly covered. Comedy, karaoke, games, markets and parks were added late, so the corpus holds far fewer of them than it does restaurants and bars. The planner refuses to offer a venue that does not say it is that kind of place, so these simply return fewer options rather than wrong ones.
  • Genre tagging is thin. Cuisine resolves for most restaurants, but genre only for some venues, and no venue is tagged `techno` because the source descriptions rarely name subgenres. A request for techno currently ranks electronic rooms above cabaret bars, which is better than nothing but not what was asked.
  • Nothing re-checks whether a venue still exists. A venue that closed or changed name stays in the corpus. One entry, The Brooklyn Monarch, appears to have rebranded; nothing in the system would catch that.
  • No real user has tried it yet. The strongest evidence this project could have is someone other than its author using it to plan a night and reporting back. That has not happened.

What I would build next

  1. Collect opening hours and refuse to place a stop at a closed venue.
  2. Add an events feed so a ticketed room can say what is actually on.
  3. Finish geocoding, and re-check venues on a schedule so closures surface.
  4. Richer genre tagging, so a request for techno finds a techno room.
  5. Put it in front of five people and watch where they get stuck.
